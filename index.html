Emergence and Self-Organization

Have you ever seen patterns appear in places where they seemed to come along randomly? A flock of birds turning as one across the sky. Water flowing down a river. A traffic jam that forms out of nowhere, then dissolves just as quickly. Nothing is telling any of these what to do, yet each one produces a pattern you can see and sometimes name.

Emergence is what happens when all the parts of a system produce patterns that none of the individual parts possess on their own.

Self-organization describes how those parts come together when nothing and nobody is dictating the specific pattern that forms.

In short, emergence is the pattern that comes out, while self-organization is the behavior of the system that creates it.

  • Key idea: the whole is not just the sum of its parts.
  • By definition, summing a bunch of ants together just means we have a bunch of ants. However, through self-organization, those ants work together in ways that look like patterns. For example, you may see one ant one day and then a million in a line the next. The first ant found food and carried it back to the nest while releasing a pheromone trail for other ants to follow. Rather than being told to follow the pheromone, other ants detect the chemical and follow the path on their own. When traveling back to the nest, each one reinforces the trail by adding its own pheromones, so even more ants follow the trail.
  • In this example, self-organization is the process of ants following and reinforcing the pheromone trail. Emergence is the ant line itself.

Variables: what goes in and what comes out

To study any system we have to decide what we change and what we observe. That's where input and output variables come in.

Input variables are what we manipulate or feed in.

Output variables are what we measure as a result.

  • In one of my studies (I try to understand the perceptions of space and time), the input might be target distance and the output might be the participant's estimated distance.
  • In a different study, the input might be sleep quantity and the output might be reported energy.

Here is the part that makes variables matter for dynamical systems. We do not just measure these once. We watch how they move over time. A variable that changes is what turns a static snapshot into a system in motion.

We can understand measurement types by talking about discrete versus continuous measures. Here, we're focused on how often something is measured over time. We call this the sampling rate.

  • Continuous variables are those that are sampled often enough that the measurement appears unbroken. These can include most heart rate monitors, neuro-imaging methods (fMRI), or movement sensors.
  • Discrete variables are those that are sampled infrequently enough for us to notice the temporal distance between the measurements. One example would be taking a mood questionnaire once a day.

The sampling rate decides what patterns you can see. A signal that oscillates quickly will disappear if you only sample it once a day. A signal that drifts slowly can be fully captured by hourly readings. This matters for dynamical systems because you can only study the dynamics you can actually observe.

Distinguishing States

A state is the configuration of a system at a moment in time. It is not just what the system looks like right now. It is the full picture of the system at that instant, complete enough to determine what it will do next.

States have different roles.

  • Some are stable and hold their shape even when the system is nudged by something external (attractor).
  • Some are unstable and don't want to stay there for very long (repeller).
  • Some are just passing through, like the pre-pattern values that show up before a system settles.

Distinguishing states lets us describe the behavior of a system. Is it seemingly sitting still? Is it cycling between a few patterns? Is it drifting unpredictably? Each question is really asking: what kind of state is the system in, and how does it move from one state to another?

The Role of Time

Have you ever experienced anything without the presence of time? I doubt it. Time is inherent to human life, to experience, to movement, to anything you can think of. Just as it is inherent in dynamical systems.

Without time, a dynamical system is just a static picture. Time allows us see how states evolve, how long they last, and how quickly they change.

Time is what turns a collection of snapshots into a story of change.

In short, time is defined by change in dynamical systems
